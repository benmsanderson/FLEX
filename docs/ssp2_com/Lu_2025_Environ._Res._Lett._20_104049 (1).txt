Abstract
Achieving the Paris Agreement’s 2 ◦ C target demands regionally tailored climate policies and
proven negative emission strategies. Here, we use a novel SSP2-com scenario that integrates
updated emissions trajectories, China’s carbon neutrality pledge, and mid-to-late 21st century
carbon dioxide removal (CDR) deployment to assess Earth system responses under a 2 ◦ C-aligned
pathway. Employing a state-of-the-art Earth system model, we demonstrate that SSP2-com limits
end-of-century warming to 1.87 ◦ C (below 2 ◦ C), 0.51 ◦ C and 1.47 ◦ C lower than SSP2-4.5 and
SSP3-7.0, respectively. Despite a reduction in global carbon sinks, SSP2-com’s stringent mitigation
avoids 0.08 ◦ C and 0.29 ◦ C of warming relative to SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0, respectively, attributed to
China’s accelerated decarbonization. Our findings suggest that combining ambitious regional
action (e.g. China’s 2060 net-zero goal) with global CDR deployment provides a critical pathway to
stabilize below 2 ◦ C, yet underscores unresolved risks from uneven cooling efficacy and ecosystem
feedbacks. This work advances scenario design by reconciling national climate pledges with global
targets, offering actionable insights for policymakers to prioritize equity-driven mitigation.

1. Introduction
Since the pre-industrial era (1750 baseline of
∼278 ppm), anthropogenic carbon dioxide (CO2 )
emissions have driven a 144.45 ppm increase in atmospheric CO2 concentration as of 2024, representing
a 52% cumulative growth (Friedlingstein et al 2025).
The unprecedented CO2 growth over the past decade
has triggered a 1.19 ◦ C global surface air temperature (GSAT) rise for the 2014–2023 average relative to
1850–1900 (Forster et al 2024), destabilizing Earth
system components through Arctic amplification

and ocean heat content accumulation (Screen 2014,
Cheng et al 2024, Oziel et al 2025). Emerging evidence
suggests that global warming is leading to more frequent and more severe heatwaves, drought and rainfall extremes (Knutson et al 2019, Perkins-Kirkpatrick
and Lewis 2020, Fowler et al 2021, Seneviratne et al
2021), underscoring the unexpected risks of delayed
mitigation.
The Paris Agreement’s global temperature control targets (2 ◦ C with 1.5 ◦ C aspiration) necessitate
enhanced mitigation strategies (IPCC 2023), including acceleration in renewable energy expansion,

improvement in energy efficiency, and implementation of carbon dioxide removal (CDR) technologies (Brunner et al 2024, Lamb et al 2024) to actively
reduce atmospheric CO2 concentrations. Nationally
Determined Contributions are also required to be
submitted to facilitate global emissions reductions
(Meinshausen et al 2022). China’s carbon neutrality pledge-peaking anthropogenic CO2 emissions by
2030 and achieving net-zero by 2060—has significant
implications for the global carbon budget and temperature stabilization (Li et al 2022, Zhang et al 2025).
Despite the urgent need for reliable climate
projections to inform mitigation and adaptation
strategies, existing climate models often fall short
in accurately representing the complexities of
regional climate actions and their global implications
(Zickfeld et al 2023, Eyring et al 2024, van Vuuren et al
2025). The shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs),
while providing a framework for exploring future
socioeconomic and emissions scenarios, frequently
deviate from actual emissions trends (Hausfather
and Peters 2020) and inadequately account for specific regional commitments, such as China’s carbon
neutrality pledge. This gap highlights the necessity
for scenarios that more faithfully integrate national
climate polices with global mitigation targets, thereby
enhancing the accuracy and relevance of climate
projections.
To reduce climate projection uncertainties, the
SSP2-com scenario–an integrated scenario derived
from SSP2 and combining diverse tools, methodologies, and national-regional-global emission data–
is developed to assess the feasibility of future emission pathways for achieving the 2 ◦ C target (Zhong
et al 2025a). This scenario addresses the limitations
of existing SSP pathways in representing plausible climate policies. Under this scenario, global anthropogenic CO2 emissions will decline to achieve net-zero
by 2080, followed by net-negative emissions throughout the rest of the century (figure S1). China’s carbon
neutrality trajectory includes the implementation of
renewable energy and phase-out of fossil fuel use,
accelerated electrification, and deployment of CDR
technologies such as bioenergy with carbon capture
and storage after the 2070s. These policy measures
provide a renewed and urgent need to reassess global
CO2 mitigation pathways and their effects on climate.
This study employs a fully coupled Earth system
model Beijing Climate Center Earth System Model
version 1 (BCC-ESM1) (Wu et al 2020) that includes
interactive carbon cycles to quantify the changes in
GSAT and carbon uptakes under the SSP2-com scenario, and explores the contribution of China’s carbon neutrality and global CDR in the mid-to-late
21st century towards achieving the 2 ◦ C target of
the Paris Agreement. The findings from this study
could provide policymarkers with critical insights

2

into the potential impact of regional climate actions
on global climate outcomes, emphasizing the importance of equitable, globally coordinated policies to
address uneven climate impacts and ensure ecosystem
resilience.

2. Methods
2.1. The BCC-ESM1 model
The BCC-ESM1 is a state-of-the-art Earth system model developed by China Meteorological
Administration for advancing climate research and
projections (Wu et al 2020). This model has demonstrated robust carbon cycle feedback simulations
in recent intercomparisons. As a key contributor
to the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project
Phase 6 (CMIP6), this fully coupled model integrates atmospheric, oceanic, terrestrial, and cryospheric components with biogeochemical cycles to
simulate complex Earth system interactions. It features a modular design incorporating atmospheric
dynamics, ocean physics, land surface processes,
and interactive carbon cycle modeling. The atmospheric component has a horizontal resolution of
approximately 2.8◦ latitude by 2.8◦ longitude and
is able to simulate global atmospheric composition and aerosols from anthropogenic emissions.
The model can reproduce reasonably the historical
global warming trend (figure S2). BCC-ESM1 excels
in projecting long-term climate trends under various
emission scenarios (SSPs), assessing climate-carbon
feedback mechanisms, and quantifying anthropogenic impacts on global systems. The model’s outputs support IPCC sixth assessment report (AR6)
and provide critical insights for climate mitigation
policymaking.

2.2. Scenario and simulation description
Table 1 summaries the scenarios and the variants
for sensitivity analysis. Three traditional SSP scenarios were used as reference in this study, namely
SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0 (O’Neill et al 2016).
SSP1-2.6 represents a low-emission scenario combining sustainable development with strong climate mitigation. It requires net-negative CO2 emissions post
2050 and represents rapid renewable energy adoption
and global cooperation. SSP2-4.5 represents a moderate emissions pathway blending gradual development
with incomplete climate action. It is characterized by
uneven regional progress and delayed decarbonization. SSP3-7.0 represents a high-emission future featuring regional rivalry and limited climate efforts. It
is driven by fossil-fuel dependency and population
growth, with amplified climate extremes and ecosystem stress.

The SSP2-com scenario is constructed within an
interdisciplinary and multi-model framework specifically designed to bridge the gap between existing emissions scenarios and the evolving reality of
global decarbonization efforts, particularly focusing on China’s ambitious carbon neutrality commitment (Zhang et al 2025, Zhong et al 2025a).
Unlike traditional SSP scenarios, which are based on
broader socio-economic narratives, SSP2-com is fundamentally grounded in a bottom-up, sector-specific
approach. It integrates the latest emissions inventories and a projected pathway for China to achieve
net-zero CO2 emissions from energy combustion and
industrial processes by 2060, taking into account natural carbon sinks, subsequently scaling this national
ambition to global and regional levels while maintaining consistency across national, provincial, and highresolution grid scales. This granular data integration,
sourced from both the AR6 scenario database and
China-specific models, ensures robust departmental
and regional emission trajectory alignment.
Key assumptions underlying SSP2-com diverge
significantly from both SSP2-4.5 and SSP1-2.6. While
SSP2-4.5 assumes a moderate socio-economic development pathway with radiative forcing reaching
4.5 W m−2 by 2100, and SSP1-2.6 represents a sustainable development pathway with stringent mitigation leading to 2.6 W m−2 , SSP2-com operates within the broader SSP2 ‘middle-of-the-road’

SSP2-com also explicitly incorporates harmonized trajectories for all major green house gases
(GHGs) other than CO2 and air pollutants. NonCO2 GHGs (CH4 , N2 O, F-gases) and aerosol emissions (SO2 , NOx , BC, OC, etc) are projected using the

In contrast to the static nature of traditional
SSP scenarios, SSP2-com introduces a dynamic and
iteratively improving framework, which allows for
semi-annual updates of regional and national emission pathways. This adaptability ensures the scenario
remains relevant and reflective of the rapidly evolving
global energy transition landscape, providing a more
responsive and policy-relevant tool for climate modeling and impact assessments than the comparatively
less frequently updated SSP2-4.5 and SSP1-2.6 scenarios. In this study, SSP2-com version 202 506 is used.
In addition, three variations of the SSP2-com
scenario are assessed in the context of sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of China’s carbon neutrality pathway and global mid-to-late 21st century CDR
deployment (table 1). SSP2-com (CN245) and SSP2com (CN370) are designed to evaluate the contribution of China’s carbon neutrality pledge on global
warming mitigation. Both build upon SSP2-com but
incorporate distinct carbon emission trajectories for
China: SSP2-com (CN245) aligns with the emission
pathway of SSP2-4.5, while SSP2-com (CN370) follows that of SSP3-7.0. Outside of China, emission trajectories remain consistent with the SSP2-com framework. SSP2-com (no CDR) is derived from SSP2-com
without negative emissions for all regions. The emission trajectories of the two remain nearly identical
until 2065. However, due to the absence of CDR
measures, SSP2-com (no CDR) exhibits persistently
higher emissions than SSP2-com in the latter half of
the century (figure S1).
All future climate projections presented in this
study are generated using the BCC-ESM1 model, simulating the climate from 2021 to 2100. Future land use
data are from the Scenario Model Intercomparison
Project for CMIP6. SSP2-com and its variants use the
same land use data as SSP2-4.5. To account for the
inherent variability and uncertainty in climate simulations, we conducted three model runs for each scenario with slightly differing initial conditions. The results reported in this study for all climate variables are
derived from the average of these three model runs.
This helps to reduce the influence of internal climate
variability and provides a more robust and representative estimate of the projected climate changes.

3. Results
3.1. Projected global warming levels
The global mean CO2 concentrations simulated
by BCC-ESM1 vary significantly among scenarios

4

(figure 1(a)). Under the SSP3-7.0 scenario, CO2
concentrations continue to rise at an accelerating
rate, reaching approximately 750 ppm by the end
of the century. The SSP2-4.5 scenario presents a
much slower increase rate of CO2 levels, which stabilize with an average of 542 ppm in the long-term
period (2081–2100). Under the low-emission scenarios SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-com, CO2 concentrations
peak around mid-century and then decline, reaching
long-term averages of 436 ppm and 444 ppm, respectively. Although SSP2-com has a mid-century CO2
concentration that is about 13 ppm higher than SSP12.6, it declines more sharply in the second half of the
century, with the difference between the two scenarios narrowing to just 2 ppm by 2100. Furthermore,
SSP2-com reduces atmospheric CO2 by 98 ppm and
325 ppm by the end of the century compared to SSP24.5 and SSP3-7.0, respectively.
In this study, GSAT continues to rise before 2050
under all four scenarios, albeit at different rates
(figure 1(b)). After 2050, the temperature stabilizes or
even slightly decreases under the low-emission SSP12.6 and SSP2-com scenarios, while SSP2-4.5 maintains a gradual warming trend. In contrast, SSP37.0 continues to present a rapid warming trajectory,
indicating an increased climate risks from prolonged
high emissions. The close correspondence between
the CO2 trajectory and the temperature response
once again confirms the dominant role of CO2 in
driving global warming through the radiative forcing
mechanism (Shine 2000).
According to Lee et al (2021), the mean GSAT
for the period 2081–2100 is very likely to be 1.3 ◦ C–
2.4 ◦ C, 2.1 ◦ C–3.5 ◦ C, and 2.8 ◦ C–4.6 ◦ C higher than
that for the period 1850–1900 under the SSP1-2.6,
SSP2-4.5, and SSP3-7.0 scenarios, respectively. In our
study, the projected GSAT rise for the period 2081–
2100 is 1.59 ◦ C, 2.38 ◦ C, and 3.34 ◦ C under SSP1-2.6,
SSP2-4.5, and SSP3-7.0 scenarios, respectively. These
results are consistent with those of Lee et al (2021).
Notably, SSP2-com achieves a global warming level of
1.87 ◦ C in the long term, which lies in between SSP24.5 and SSP1-2.6, and below 2 ◦ C.

3.2. Future changes in land and ocean carbon
uptakes
Since carbon uptake by land and ocean is driven
primarily by increases in atmospheric CO2 , the evolution of land and ocean carbon sinks varies significantly across scenarios (figure 3). The absolute value
of the carbon sink is larger under scenarios with
greater increases in atmospheric CO2 (e.g. SSP3-7.0
and SSP2-4.5), while it is smaller under scenarios
with reduced CO2 in the 21st century (e.g. SSP1-2.6
and SSP2-com). The land and ocean carbon sinks are
quantitatively similar, but the interannual variability
of land carbon uptake is much larger (figure 3(b)).
Under the SSP2-com scenario, the atmosphereocean carbon exchange is very close to SSP1-2.6,
slightly above SSP1-2.6 levels from 2021 to 2075, but
drops below SSP1-2.6 by 2100, with a net exchange
of less than 1 PgC yr−1 (figure 3(a)). In 2021, global
terrestrial ecosystems act as carbon sinks, uptaking
∼3 PgC yr−1 . The decadal trends show that under
SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0, the terrestrial carbon sink
increases, reaching 4 PgC yr−1 by the end of the 21st
century (figure 3(b)). The SSP2-com terrestrial carbon sink closely matches SSP1-2.6, slightly exceeding it before 2060, but then falling below it and
approaching zero net exchange by 2100. This divergence stems from the lower global warming under
SSP2-com and SSP1-2.6 compared to higher emission
scenarios, which reduce vegetation net primary productivity and weakens carbon sequestration.
Presently, global carbon sources and sinks exhibit
distinct spatial patterns shaped by natural processes (figure 4(a)). In BCC-ESM1, CO2 fluxes from

6

land/ocean to atmosphere indicate that low-latitude
continental regions function as major carbon sinks,
with fluxes of approximately 50–250 gC m−2 yr−1 ,
whereas mid- to high-latitude continental regions act
as moderate to strong carbon sources, with fluxes ranging from 25 to 100 gC m−2 yr−1 . In contrast, oceanic
regions display the opposite distribution: low-latitude
oceans function as carbon sources, while mid- to
high-latitude oceans serve as carbon sinks.
Figures 4(b)–(e) illustrate the projected geographical patterns of future changes in CO2 fluxes
compared to the present-day baseline. The SSP12.6 and SSP2-com scenarios demonstrate comparable patterns: carbon sinks are expected to intensify
in Western Europe, while low-latitude tropical
regions—particularly the Amazon Basin and West
Africa—show a decline in carbon sink strength.
Concurrently, northern mid- to high-latitude continental areas are expected to experience an increase
in carbon sources. Compared to SSP1-2.6, the SSP2com scenario indicates a greater reduction in carbon
sequestration capacity. This shift may be attributed to
increased plant respiration induced by global warming. Under low emission scenarios, relatively low
atmospheric CO2 concentrations provide insufficient
stimulation for photosynthesis, resulting in a decline
in net primary productivity and, consequently, a
diminished carbon uptake capacity (Schimel et al
2014, Keenan et al 2023).
Under high emission scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and
SSP3-7.0), carbon sink strength is expected to
increase in North America, Western Europe, and
low-latitude continental regions. This enhancement is primarily driven by CO2 fertilization and
temperature-induced increases in photosynthesis,
which together enhance terrestrial carbon sinks.

3.4. GSAT responses to CDR
In the case of no global adoption of CDR technology in the mid-to-late 21st century, i.e. SSP2-com
(no CDR), the average GSAT increase from 2081 to
2100 is 1.94 ◦ C, which is 0.07 ◦ C higher than the
SSP2-com scenario (figure 5(a)). This result indicates that the implementation of CDR is beneficial to

4. Conclusions

Conflict of interest

This study demonstrates that the SSP2-com scenario, integrating China’s carbon neutrality pledge
and mid-to-late 21st century global CDR deployment, provides a viable pathway to stabilize global
warming below 2 ◦ C. By 2081–2100, SSP2-com limits end-of-century warming to 1.87 ◦ C–0.51 ◦ C and
1.47 ◦ C lower than SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0, respectively. The scenario’s effectiveness stems from stringent regional mitigation, particularly China’s accelerated decarbonization, which reduces global temperature rise by 0.08 ◦ C and 0.29 ◦ C compared to
the SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0 scenarios with delayed
action, respectively. Furthermore, CDR implementation contributes an additional 0.07 ◦ C cooling, with
pronounced efficacy at high latitudes, partially mitigating Arctic amplification.
Despite these advancements, SSP2-com reveals
critical unresolved risks. While terrestrial and ocean
carbon sinks decline under low-emission pathways
due to reduced CO2 fertilization and warmingdriven ecosystem feedbacks, regional disparities in
cooling efficacy persist. For instance, mid- to lowlatitude oceans exhibit weaker responses to CDR,
and Antarctic regions paradoxically warm under
stringent mitigation. These findings underscore the
need for equitable, globally coordinated policies to
address uneven climate impacts and ensure ecosystem
resilience.
Our work advances scenario design by harmonizing national commitments with global targets,
emphasizing the dual necessity of immediate regional
action and scalable CDR technologies. Policymakers
must prioritize equity-driven strategies to bridge the
gap between mitigation ambition and implementation, while addressing uncertainties in carbon cycle
feedbacks and regional climate responses. This study
